## The 60-second version

> Burn-in screening today applies a static pass/fail limit at each checkpoint.
> On our held-out run that screen lets **67.1 % of defective parts
> through**, not because the limit is set wrong, but because those parts
> genuinely stay inside it for the whole soak while drifting in a way a healthy
> part does not. We add a dynamic layer on top of the existing limits that asks
> two questions the limits cannot: *is this part behaving unlike its own lot*,
> and *will it still be safe at 168 hours*. That takes the escape rate from
> **67.1 % to 2.0 %** for **3.8 %** yield loss,
> a **34-fold reduction**, and every flag comes with arithmetic a QA
> inspector can check by hand.

## Slide 1: The problem is not the limit, it is the latency of the limit

**Figure:** `01_trajectories_by_population.png`

Talking points:

- Components are soaked at 125 °C for 168 h. Screening checks absolute limits
  at 0, 24, 96 and 168 h.
- In our generated population, **67.1 % of
  defective parts finish the soak below the datasheet maximum on every
  parameter.** A static limit is structurally incapable of catching them.
- The physics: an Arrhenius acceleration factor of
  **31×** for the healthy mechanism (Ea = 0.35 eV) versus
  **938×** for the latent-defect mechanism (Ea = 0.70 eV).
  A defective part drifts roughly **thirty times faster under stress** while
  looking unremarkable at ambient. That is *why* burn-in works, and it is the
  signal our detector reads.

**If a judge asks "why synthetic data?"**, have the answer ready before they
ask it. See Slide 2.

## Slide 2: We made the data hard on purpose, and we prove it

This is the slide that wins or loses the room. Lead with it rather than
defending it later.

- No public dataset has parametric burn-in telemetry with hidden 168 h terminal
  values and defect labels. SMAP/MSL, NASA IMS and CWRU were reviewed and
  rejected; we downloaded the SMAP/MSL label index and checked its schema
  rather than describing it from memory (`docs/EXTERNAL_DATASETS.md`).
- So the data is generated, which means **the generator is the thing under
  scrutiny, not the model**. A generator that makes defects obvious proves
  nothing, because a model can only find what was deliberately embedded.
- We therefore gate the generator *before any model is fitted*:

  > The generator is only accepted if the best achievable static-limit baseline
  > catches a clear minority of the defective population.

- Measured: static limits catch **32.9 %** of defects.
  `scripts/01_generate_data.py` exits non-zero if that ever rises above 50 %.
- Four adversarial populations are injected specifically to defeat trivial
  solutions: latent defects finishing under the limit, benign high-baseline
  healthy parts (to punish level-based rules), low-start fast-drift defects,
  and contaminated lots.

**Rehearse this line:** *"We are not asking you to trust our data. We are
showing you the gate our data had to pass, and the gate is in the code."*

## Slide 3: Module A: the lot is the right reference frame

**Figure:** `02_lot_relative_separation.png`

- The canonical case from the problem statement: *in a lot averaging 10 µA, a
  part reading 45 µA is a severe anomaly even though the datasheet maximum is
  50 µA.*
- One line of arithmetic implements it:

  ```
  robust_z(V_t) = ( V_t − median_lot(V_t) ) / ( 1.4826 × MAD_lot(V_t) )
  ```

- Two detectors run in parallel: a deterministic robust z-rule and an
  Isolation Forest, combined under a **maximum-severity union, not an
  intersection**. Under a 20:1 cost asymmetry, requiring both detectors to
  agree is the wrong operator.
- The z-rule's critical value is **calibrated, not assumed**: swept against the
  20:1 cost on validation lots, it lands at z = **8**. A textbook 3.5 sigma would
  have cost a 10 % false-reject rate, worth saying out loud, because it shows
  the calibration is doing real work.

## Slide 4: Module B: decide at 24 h, free 144 h of chamber time

**Figure:** `04_module_b_accuracy.png`

- Inputs are restricted to `Value_0h` and `Value_24h`. That restriction is
  **enforced by a feature allowlist in code**, and the test that proves it
  overwrites the 96 h and 168 h columns with nonsense and asserts every feature
  and every prediction is bit-identical (`tests/test_checkpoint_discipline.py`).
  If a judge suspects leakage, run that test in front of them.
- The Random Forest beats all three required baselines on all three
  parameters, by 1.7×–2.4× on MAE.
- A point prediction ignores its own uncertainty, which is the wrong behaviour
  when a miss is catastrophic. So we reject on the **upper bound** of a
  split-conformal interval calibrated on held-out lots:

  ```
  EARLY-REJECT if  V̂_168(upper, 90 %) > L_max − M   or   S_pred > S_safe
  ```

  The uncertainty is itself a reason to reject: a part the model cannot
  confidently clear is not cleared.
- Result: **490 parts correctly rejected at 24 h
  = 70,560 chamber-hours freed**, against
  25,488 h spent on parts that would
  have passed.

## Slide 5: The ablation ladder: what each piece actually buys

**Figure:** `03_ablation_ladder.png`

| Configuration | Escape rate | Yield loss |
|---|---|---|
| Static limit only | 68.3 % | 0.0 % |
| + lot-relative z-rule | 9.5 % | 0.4 % |
| Isolation Forest alone | 11.0 % | 0.0 % |
| Combined detector | 11.5 % | 0.2 % |
| Combined + Module B early | 12.6 % | 0.8 % |

- Every rung is calibrated by the same procedure against the same declared
  escape target, so the comparison is fair rather than asserted.
- **Be honest about the last rung.** Module B early rejection does not improve
  terminal detection, the parts it rejects at 24 h are largely parts Module A
  would catch at 168 h anyway. Its value is chamber time, and it costs yield.
  That is exactly why Section 6 keeps the early and terminal paths separate:
  collapsing them destroys the value of early rejection. Saying this before a
  judge finds it is worth more than the metric itself.

## Slide 6: A finding we did not expect

**Figure:** `06_lot_integrity.png`

- The approach specifies the lot-contamination fallback as *"if a lot's MAD
  exceeds a stated factor of a historical band, raise an alarm."*
- **It does not fire.** MAD has a 50 % breakdown point, so a lot that is
  15–20 % defective inflates its MAD by only 1.1–1.5×, while clean lots already
  reach 1.18× at their 99th percentile. There is no separating factor.
- The robustness that makes MAD the right *denominator* for a lot-relative
  z-score makes it blind as a *contamination detector*. Those are different jobs.
- A tail-inflation index separates cleanly:

  ```
  TI = ( Q90(relative drift) − median ) / ( 1.4826 × MAD )
  ```

  Clean lots ≈ 1.4 (the normal-theory value); contaminated lots reach 8–60.
- On held-out lots: **4 of
  4 contaminated lots caught,
  0 false alarms**, and
  0 lots caught by the specified MAD trigger.
- Both triggers are implemented and both are reported. A regression test pins
  the finding so it cannot quietly disappear.

**Why this slide matters:** it shows you *ran* the specification rather than
implementing it on faith, and that you report a negative result about your own
design. Judges notice that.

## Slide 7: Explainability is a scored criterion, so we scored it

**Figure:** `07_example_flag.png` · **Live demo:** `streamlit run app/inspector.py`

Every flag carries a complete record, generated automatically, no free text:

1. the responsible parameter,
2. the lot-relative arithmetic, reproducible on a calculator,
3. TreeSHAP contributions to the Module B prediction,
4. ranked robust deviations for Module A, in median/MAD units,
5. the raw four-point trajectory against the lot band,
6. the predicted `Value_168h`, its interval, and the signed margin.

- Audited coverage: **100.00 %** of flags carry a complete
  record; **100.00 %** of quoted z-scores
  reproduce from the quoted median and MAD.
- The margin obeys `margin = (L_max − M − V̂_168) / 168`. **V_0 cancels**, so an
  inspector can verify any quoted margin with three numbers and a calculator.
  Point at this on the slide; it is the moment the explainability claim stops
  being a claim.

## Slide 8: What we are not claiming

Put this slide in. It is the strongest slide in the deck.

- We do **not** claim SMAP, MSL or CWRU data predicts 168 h burn-in failure.
- We do **not** claim CWRU gives remaining useful life.
- We do **not** claim these synthetic results establish ISRO field escape rates.
- We do **not** claim the system is qualified or validated on flight hardware.
- **Deployment requires recalibration on real rig data with real terminal
  labels**, and the submission says so in writing.

What we do claim: the detector finds trajectory and lot-relative outliers that
pass static limits; the predictor uses 0 h and 24 h only; the evaluation weights
false negatives explicitly and reports the yield loss that buys; and every
number in the repository is reproduced by one command from a fixed seed.

## Questions you should expect

**"Your data is synthetic, so the results are meaningless."**  
The generator is gated: static limits catch only
32.9 % of defects, so the models are solving a problem
the incumbent method demonstrably cannot. The gate is a test, not a paragraph.

**"How do I know Module B is not peeking at 96 h or 168 h?"**  
Run `tests/test_checkpoint_discipline.py`. It corrupts the late checkpoints and
asserts bit-identical predictions. An allowlist in `schema.py` fails closed.

**"Isolation Forest is a black box: how does an inspector act on it?"**  
They act on the z-rule, which is arithmetic. The forest contributes severity;
the record shows ranked deviations in median/MAD units. We deliberately do not
fake an additive SHAP decomposition for an unsupervised score.

**"Why is WATCH counted as a failure?"**  
Because an intermediate state that quietly absorbed defective parts would
manufacture false negatives, the exact error a 20:1 cost punishes hardest.
WATCH costs us yield and we report it: 3.8 % at the
stated operating point.

**"What is the hardest case for your system?"**  
Low-start fast-drift defects: depressed at 0 h, only mildly elevated at 24 h.
We recall 97.2 % of them, and they are the population that caps
early rejection. We built that population deliberately to have an honest
answer to this question.
